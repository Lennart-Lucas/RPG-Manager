## Description

The Norcaine, called dark elves throughout the kingdoms of the light, are a long-lived people descended from the elves by divine alteration rather than natural lineage. They are distinguished by bleak, drained skin, jet-black hair, and a culture organised almost entirely around warfare, magic, and the worship of the night god [[RPG Manager/Characters/Nor]]. Once the most feared of the participants in the War of the Six Crowns, they hold today the coastal realm of Shall, a fortified civilisation of obsidian towers, hereditary houses, and slave labour.
They are not to be confused with the sunlit elves of the western forests, from whose essence they were made, and whom they regard as the single object of a religious hatred older than any surviving kingdom.

# Etymology and names
Norcaine is formed from the divine name [[RPG Manager/Characters/Nor]] with the collective suffix -caine, "those given to" — literally the given-to-Nor. It is used as both noun and adjective and does not inflect for number; a single Norcaine and the whole people take the same word, a grammatical fact their poets treat as doctrine.
The term dark elf is a translation of the phrase used by human chroniclers during the War of the Six Crowns, and is resented by the Norcaine, who consider it a remark about their skin rather than their allegiance.

# Biology and appearance
The Norcaine are elves in every structural respect: slender, long-limbed, sharp-featured, slow to age, with lifespans measured in centuries. What distinguishes them is colouration and constitution.
Skin is bleak and drained — slate grey through ash and bone to a cold bluish white. It does not tan; prolonged sunlight blisters and eventually scars, which is the practical origin of their nocturnal habits and heavy hooded dress. Hair is uniformly jet black across every bloodline, worn long and bound in patterns that declare house and rank. Eyes are pale — silver, grey, or colourless — and catch light in darkness, a trait attributed to Nor's touch.
They run cold. Resting body temperature is several degrees below a human's, their hands are notoriously chill, and they endure hard frost with indifference while suffering badly in heat. They are also markedly resistant to fear, pain, and fatigue, which their own scholars ascribe to the manner of their making: they were not born as a people but composed as one, and something of the artisan's intent remains in them.

# History
## The Making
According to the canonical account as recited nightly in the temples of Shall, [[RPG Manager/Characters/Nor]], the Silverweaver, god of night and lord of the silver disc of the moon, was the first God to descend to the earth and tell the races of the gods and their duties. In time they forgot his teachings and turned away from the cold master of darkness, seeking instead the gods of light and the sun. Disappointed and spurned, [[RPG Manager/Characters/Nor]] was quick to side with the renegade [[RPG Manager/Characters/Zarach]] when the gods began to disunite — [[RPG Manager/Characters/Zarach]], once the Guardian Ulm the Caretaker, who had abandoned his charge in disgust at the task the Father of the gods [[RPG Manager/Characters/Aonir]] had set him.
As a token of gratitude, [[RPG Manager/Characters/Zarach]] made his dark brother a following. He did not create it from nothing. He took the elves, already made, and reworked them considerably — cooling the essence, sharpening it, stripping out what he judged to be sentiment. From that work came the Norcaine: the most beautiful, most perfect, and most deadly of his creations. They have prayed to [[RPG Manager/Characters/Nor]] ever since, but no Norcaine forgets whose hands shaped them, and the two devotions have never sat easily together.

## The War of the Six Crowns
From Urgath the Norcaine crossed to [[RPG Manager/Locations/Valmoria]] with their brothers in arms, the orcs and trolls. It is agreed on both sides that their sharp intellect and discipline nearly won them the war. Where orc hosts broke and reformed by instinct, Norcaine armies manoeuvred; where troll siege-work was brute mass, Norcaine sappers worked to plan. The war ended not in their defeat but in exhaustion.

## The founding of Shall
Rather than withdraw, the Norcaine stayed and founded the realm of Shall on the nebulous northern coast — a country of sea-fog, black rock, and short days. There they raised the towers of obsidian for which they are known, bound the war-companies of the crossing into the great houses as institutions of state, and forged the heavy armour and pale moonsilver blades that remain their most recognisable export and their most recognisable threat. Those weapons have since been carried through uncounted cruel battles against the races of the light.

# Society
Norcaine society has no citizens. It has houses, and it has those who belong to none.
The house is the unit of everything: property, law, debt, marriage, worship, military obligation, and vengeance. A Norcaine does not own land — a house does, and permits its use. A Norcaine does not commit a crime against another Norcaine — a house incurs a debt against a house, and the debt is settled between them, sometimes without either of the individuals involved being consulted. The Council that governs Shall is not a body of officeholders but a table of house-heads, and its authority extends exactly as far as the houses seated at it agree that it should, which is not always very far.
Membership is by blood, and is for life. It is possible to be raised into a house and possible to be stripped of one, but not possible to change houses, and the Norcaine consider the idea faintly obscene.

## The great houses
Six houses are counted great. Precedence among them is disputed and has been settled by war twice.
Each great house carries a trademark — a discipline it perfected, propagated, and is known throughout the world for. This is a matter of reputation and pedigree, not monopoly. A Drach'on daughter may be a sorceress of the first rank and a Vesperine son may spend his life in a smithy; the trademark says what the house is for, what its schools teach best, and what its enemies expect to face, not what its members are permitted to become. Norcaine outside a house's tradition study it anyway, and often surpass it. What they cannot do is claim it.
Drach'on — steel and discipline. The martial tradition: the assassins of the grey schools, the battlemasters, the strategists who give Norcaine armies their manoeuvre. Drach'on holds that an honourable life is one spent fighting the Light, keeps the oldest and cruellest training establishments in Shall, and regards every other house as a merchant of some kind.
Archon — black magic and governance. The towers, the academies, the necromantic schools from which the screams of the servants echo day and night. Archon produces the realm's warlocks and most of its administrators, and is the house most likely to win an argument it did not enter. Drach'on considers it a house of faithless careerists, which Archon does not dispute so much as fail to understand as an insult.
Sinistra — weapons and black magic used together. The darkblades and death knights, whose armour trembles with the power worked into it, and who fight and cast as a single discipline rather than two. Where death is Drach'on's craft and Archon's science, Sinistra treats it as worship. The house is smaller than either and considerably more dangerous per head.
Vesperine — the rites. Keepers of the moon-calendar, the liturgy, and the tribute of daughters owed to the Spiderbrood. Vesperine commands almost no soldiers and cannot be refused by anyone, because it decides which nights are auspicious for a campaign, whose marriages are recognised, and which houses are asked for girls. It is the youngest of the great houses and the one the others most want abolished.
Kal'Duran — the fog-coast and the black fleet. Blockade, raid, salt, and the entire export trade in moonsilver. Kal'Duran is the wealthiest house in Shall and the least respected, having risen through commerce during a century when the others were killing each other, a fact it is careful never to mention and the others never forget.
Vor'Ghal — the deep. Tunnel-wrights, siege-miners, and the conjurors of the great Silver Halls beneath the mountains, who call the Arachnis out of their cold hideouts and into war. Vor'Ghal is the oldest house and the smallest, keeps its own calendar, marries almost exclusively within itself, and is regarded by the rest of Shall as something between a national treasure and a problem that has not yet happened.
By convention, great houses take no particle. The name is the house.

## The lesser houses
Beneath the six are the lesser houses, of which there are several hundred in Shall alone — some ancient and merely poor, some three generations old and enormously rich, most neither. Their names carry the particle Un', "of the line of": Un'Shallach, Un'Vroth, Un'Kaireth, Un'Talach, Un'Morroch.
Almost every lesser house is sworn to a great one. The arrangement is not feudal in the human sense and involves no land. What the lesser house receives is standing: the right to have its debts pursued, its dead avenged, and — critically — its disputes carried into Elyn Muin by a champion it could never produce itself. What the great house receives is levies, revenue, marriages, votes on the Council in all but name, and the use of the lesser house's particular trade.

A few examples of the pattern:

Un'Shallach, sworn to Drach'on — duellists and martial poets; supplies more gladiators to the circle than any three other houses, and has buried most of them.
Un'Vroth, sworn to Sinistra — moonsilver smiths; the only lesser house whose head is seated at the Council table in his own right, a concession Sinistra granted once and has regretted since.
Un'Kaireth, sworn to Archon — alchemists and poisoners, and the house most frequently accused of things nobody can prove.
Un'Talach, sworn to Archon — moneylenders to the towers. Not old, not liked, and owed a great deal by people who cannot afford to have it known.
Un'Morroch, sworn to Vor'Ghal — tunnel-wrights and stone-singers, who have worked the same three mountains for nine hundred years and speak an accent the coast finds comical.

An unsworn lesser house is legally intact and practically defenceless. Some manage it for decades by being useful to everyone; when one falls, it falls quickly, and its name is not usually re-founded.

Elyn Muin

Disputes between houses too fierce for an arbitrator to settle are referred to the Council, which calls Elyn Muin — the ritual duel in the circle of drums. In most cases the disputants do not fight; each house puts forward its best fighters, chosen and trained for no other purpose. These gladiators occupy an odd position in Norcaine society: celebrated, well kept, and socially dead, since they belong to the circle rather than to a house.

The right to send a champion is the single most valuable thing a great house has to sell, and the reason the sworn system exists at all. A lesser house with a grievance and no champion has a grievance and nothing else.

The houseless

To be houseless is not to be poor. It is to be outside the mechanism entirely.

The houseless hold nothing, inherit nothing, and swear nothing. They cannot own property, hold office, marry within a house, or be entered in any house's rolls. Most consequentially, their deaths are not debts: killing a houseless Norcaine incurs no obligation to anyone, because there is no one to be obliged. The law does not protect them so much as decline to notice them.

Two paths lead there, and the Norcaine have deliberately declined to distinguish between them.

The stripped — those cast out by judgement, whether of the Council or of their own house's head. The sentence removes the name and the particle in a single stroke and is regarded as considerably worse than execution, which is why it is used.

The baseborn — those born outside a recognised union: the slave-born, the unclaimed, the children of matches Vesperine refused to sanction. They have committed nothing. They are houseless from the first breath.

Both take the name Shadowsong, as Snow is taken in the north or Sand in the south of certain human kingdoms — a surname that is really a status. The word renders a Norcaine phrase for a song heard with no singer visible: something present in the room and accounted for by nobody. That a disgraced Archon warlock and a slave's daughter carry the same name is not an oversight. It is the point. The houses maintain that there is no meaningful difference between the two, and the houseless have never entirely disagreed.

Their practical position is stranger than their legal one. Precisely because a Shadowsong's death costs nothing, the great houses find them extraordinarily useful — as agents, killers, couriers, and negotiators in matters where a house cannot be seen to act. A Shadowsong hired to do murder can be disowned in a sentence, because there is nothing to disown. Several of the more consequential events in the recent history of Shall were carried out by people the record does not name, and every house at the Council table knows it, and the arrangement continues.

Some Shadowsong take service. Some form companies on the fog-coast that are mercenary bands in all but name and are hired chiefly by Kal'Duran. A few accumulate real power, which the houses tolerate for exactly as long as it is aimed outward.

It is possible, rarely, to be claimed — raised into a house by its head, whereupon the name is struck and the particle granted. This is done perhaps once in a generation, usually to legitimise a bastard whose talents have become embarrassing to waste. Those raised are watched forever. A handful refuse the offer and keep the name, which the Norcaine find genuinely incomprehensible and which has produced most of their better drama.

Vesperine forbids the offering of Shadowsong daughters to the Spiderbrood tribute in place of a house's own. Houses attempt it regularly. Vesperine's stated grounds are that the god notices.

Slavery

The Skerg are a simple, timid people of the swamps of Urgath. When the orcs, trolls and Norcaine reached their shores the Skerg welcomed them as gods, and it did not take long for their weakness to be seen through. The orcs and trolls hunted them for sport; the Norcaine found a more useful fate, and have bred them as slaves ever since. They suffer immensely, but generations of submission have left their broken minds without resistance. The larger and cleverer Tar-Skerg are set to heavy or difficult work and are fairly decent craftsmen, producing many of the tools and trinkets of Norcaine domestic life.

Slaves belong to houses, never to individuals. A Skerg's standing derives from its owning house exactly as a Norcaine's does, with the result that a Drach'on slave outranks an unsworn lesser house's freeborn servant in every practical situation, and everyone involved knows it.

Religion
Nor, the Silverweaver

[[RPG Manager/Characters/Nor]] is worshipped as lord of night, the moon, cold, and silence. Norcaine days and nights alike are spent in prayer to him and in the destruction of the light-seeking worms whose gods forced him into exile. His rites are nocturnal, wordless in their higher forms, and ordered by the phases of the moon. His priesthood holds that the ruin of the light-worshipping peoples is not conquest but restitution — the correction of an old theft.

The Spiderbrood

[[RPG Manager/Characters/Nor]], having been given followers rather than making them, thirsted to create a creature of his own. Knowing he could never succeed in making a people, he resolved instead to give his dark children an ally who would sow fear and doubt in the hearts of all who opposed them.

Year after year he demands from his subjects the most beautiful of their maidens, carries them away to his silver-spun home, and there weaves body and soul together with his own essence. What returns is the pale Spiderbrood — half woman, half spider, wholly filled with Nor's cold hatred. The Norcaine still give up their daughters to the ritual and receive in return the bond with Nor's poisonous brood, who drive into the ranks of the Light hissing, to drink. Born of the darkest magic, the brood is practically immune to it; only the madness inside them leaves their minds open to those who know how to reach it.

The tribute is assessed by house. Which houses are asked, and how often, is decided by Vesperine, and is the most reliable instrument of political pressure in Shall.

Though the night god has been driven from the world, his misbegotten children still dwell in it, waiting in the pale silver light of the moon for the father they pray to with husky voices reaching into the barren night. In the great Silver Halls beneath the mountains, Norcaine sorcerers weave long conjurations to call the Arachnis — the brood's deep-dwelling kin — out of their cold hideouts and into war.

Zarach, and the Fial Darg

Zarach is the maker but not the master, and outside the Crimson Empire his worship among the Norcaine is a quiet minority tradition, practised on the principle that a people should not forget whose hands shaped them. Orthodox doctrine treats this as ingratitude to the god who received them; Zarachite doctrine treats orthodoxy as the ingratitude. The argument has never been settled without blood.

The Fial Darg, the Princes of Darkness, were Zarach's lieutenants in the War of the Six Crowns. The death knights of Sinistra were appointed their guardians, and hold themselves still bound by an ancient oath never to falter, sworn to masters long absent from the world. They consider their talent for magic a gift from the Fial Darg, and by custom stand at the centre of any attack and are the last to remain in any defence.
